Green tea beverages are food-format sources of caffeine, catechins such as [[EGCG]], and [[L-theanine]]. They are usually safer than concentrated green tea extract supplements, but large daily volumes can still matter for sleep, anxiety, heart rate, IBS, and medication stacking.

## Caffeine range

Green tea caffeine content varies by product, leaf amount, extraction, bottle size, and dilution. Public reference tables put brewed green tea around 29-50 mg caffeine per 237-250 mL serving.

Practical estimate:

- 700 mL/day: roughly 85-150 mg caffeine if brewed-tea equivalent.
- 1 L/day: roughly 120-210 mg caffeine if brewed-tea equivalent.
- Bottled ready-to-drink products may be lower or higher, so product label caffeine per 100 mL or per bottle is the better source.

This is below the FDA's general 400 mg/day adult reference level, but that reference is for most healthy adults and does not mean "optimal" or "sleep-safe." Chronic insomnia, stimulant medication, atomoxetine, bupropion, anxiety, and caffeine sensitivity lower the practical threshold.

## Food vs extract

Green tea consumed as a beverage has a different risk profile from high-dose green tea extract capsules. NCCIH notes no safety concerns have been reported for green tea consumed as a beverage by adults, while green tea extracts have liver safety concerns. EFSA similarly separates traditional green tea infusions from high-dose catechin supplements.

## ADHD medication stack implications

With methylphenidate, atomoxetine, or bupropion, green tea is not just hydration. It adds caffeine and can contribute to:

- higher BP/HR or palpitations
- anxiety or irritability
- appetite suppression
- afternoon crash
- sleep onset delay or more middle awakenings

Theanine in tea may soften subjective jitteriness, but it does not remove caffeine's half-life or adenosine receptor effects. For chronic insomnia, cutoff time is more important than whether the caffeine source feels "clean."

## IBS and zero-sugar products

"Zero sugar" can mean unsweetened tea, non-nutritive sweeteners, or sugar alcohols depending on the product. IBS-relevant label checks:

- sugar alcohols such as sorbitol, maltitol, xylitol, erythritol
- inulin/FOS or fiber additives
- citric acid/acidulants if reflux is present
- total caffeine if diarrhea or urgency worsens

Unsweetened brewed-style green tea is usually simpler for IBS than sweetened zero-calorie drinks, but individual tolerance still matters.
